Understanding Anal Fissures

An anal fissure is a small tear in the thin lining of the anal canal. It typically causes a sharp, cutting pain while passing stools, often followed by a burning ache that can last for hours.

Fissures are common and often start after passing a hard stool. Pain makes the anal muscle go into spasm, which reduces blood flow to the tear and can stop it from healing.

A fissure that has been present for less than about six to eight weeks is called acute, and most of these heal with diet changes, sitz baths and medication. A chronic fissure that does not heal with these measures may need a short procedure to relax the muscle and allow healing.

Common symptoms

  • Sharp or tearing pain while passing stools
  • A burning or throbbing ache that lasts for minutes to hours afterwards
  • Small amounts of bright red blood on the stool or toilet paper
  • A visible crack or small skin tag near the anus
  • Itching or irritation around the anus
  • Fear of going to the toilet, which can worsen constipation

Causes & risk factors

  • Passing hard or large stools due to constipation
  • Straining during bowel movements
  • Long-standing diarrhoea
  • Childbirth
  • High tension in the anal sphincter muscle
  • Less commonly, Crohn's disease, tuberculosis or other infections

When to see a doctor promptly

  • Pain or bleeding that has not improved after two weeks of home care
  • Heavy bleeding or passing clots
  • Fever, swelling or pus near the anus, which may indicate an abscess
  • A change in bowel habits or unexplained weight loss

Types

  • 01

    Acute fissure

    A fresh, shallow tear, usually present for less than six to eight weeks. Most heal with conservative treatment.

  • 02

    Chronic fissure

    A deeper tear that has lasted longer than six to eight weeks, often with a skin tag. It may need a procedure if medication does not help.

Treatment options

How we treat anal fissures

Every plan starts with a careful diagnosis. We recommend the least invasive option that will work for you.

  • Diet and lifestyle changes

    Non-surgical

    A high-fibre diet, plenty of water and not straining keep stools soft, which takes pressure off the tear. This is the foundation of treatment for every fissure.

  • Sitz baths

    Home care

    Sitting in warm water for 10–15 minutes a few times a day, especially after a bowel movement, relaxes the anal muscle and eases pain.

  • Medication

    Non-surgical

    Stool softeners, local anaesthetic gels and prescription ointments that relax the sphincter muscle can relieve pain and help many fissures heal over several weeks.

  • Laser treatment

    Minimally invasive

    For chronic fissures, laser can be used to treat the fissure and any associated skin tag with minimal cutting. Most patients go home the same day.

  • Lateral internal sphincterotomy

    Surgical

    A small, controlled cut is made in the internal sphincter muscle to relieve spasm and improve blood flow so the fissure can heal. It is a well-established option for chronic fissures that do not respond to other treatment.

What to expect

Your treatment, step by step

  1. STEP 01

    Consultation and examination

    Your surgeon takes a history and gently examines the area to confirm the fissure and check for other causes of pain or bleeding.

  2. STEP 02

    Trying conservative care

    Most patients first follow a plan of diet changes, sitz baths and medication. A procedure is considered if the fissure does not heal.

  3. STEP 03

    The procedure

    If needed, the fissure is treated under anaesthesia with laser or sphincterotomy. This is usually a short procedure lasting under half an hour.

  4. STEP 04

    Discharge and follow-up

    Most patients go home the same day with pain relief, stool softeners and diet advice. A follow-up visit checks that the fissure is healing.

Recovery timeline

  • First few days

    Many patients notice that the sharp pain with bowel movements eases soon after the procedure. Mild soreness is managed with tablets and sitz baths.

  • 1 week

    Most people are back to work and normal daily activities. Keeping stools soft remains important.

  • 4–6 weeks

    The fissure usually heals over this period. Your surgeon will check healing at follow-up.

FAQs

Anal Fissures: your questions answered

Can't find your answer? Call us at +91 84315 50550.

Do all anal fissures need surgery?

No. Most acute fissures heal with diet changes, sitz baths and medication. A procedure is usually considered only for chronic fissures that do not heal with these measures.

How is a fissure different from piles?

A fissure is a tear in the lining of the anus and typically causes sharp pain with bowel movements. Piles are swollen blood vessels that more often cause painless bleeding or a lump, although the two can occur together.

Will sphincterotomy affect bowel control?

A small number of patients notice minor changes in control of gas or loose stools, which often improve with time. Your surgeon will discuss this risk and whether it applies to you before the procedure.

How long does a fissure take to heal?

An acute fissure often heals within a few weeks with good care. After a procedure for a chronic fissure, healing usually takes around four to six weeks.

Can a fissure come back?

Yes, fissures can recur, particularly if constipation returns. A high-fibre diet, enough water and not straining are the best ways to prevent this.
